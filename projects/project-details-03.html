Reliability of Printed Electronics
  • Category: Research
  • Affiliation: University of Maryland, College Park
  • Sponsor: SMIEL Lab, Laboratory for Physical Sciences
  • PI: Dr. Siddhartha Das, Prof. Abhijit Dasgupta
  • Collab: Prabhat Janamanchi, Edwin Quinn (LPS), Beihan Zhao, Vishal S. Sivasankar
  • Published: 2023 – 2025 (three papers)

Printing a circuit or a sensor is now easy. Knowing how long it will keep working is not. This project brings together three pieces of work on that question: a perspective on wearable devices, long-term tests of printed carbon-nanotube sensors, and a thermal-cycling study of printed silver circuits.



A new printed sensor is usually shown working on the day it is made, in a lab. A real product has to keep working through years of heat and cold, humidity, sweat, bending and vibration. Reliability engineering asks how, where and when a device will fail, and tests that question deliberately instead of waiting to find out. This page collects my published work on the reliability of printed electronics.

Accelerated testing. Nobody can wait five years to learn whether a device lasts five years. Instead, samples are put through harsher conditions than they would normally see (bigger temperature swings, higher humidity, more bending) over and over, while their electrical behaviour is monitored. A sample "fails" when a measured value, such as electrical resistance, drifts past an agreed limit. Understanding the physics of each failure is what lets you translate cycles in a test chamber into years of real use.

1. Why reliability is the missing piece

In a first-author perspective article, Prof. Siddhartha Das and I looked at wearable lab-on-a-chip devices: skin patches that collect sweat or other body fluids and measure things like pH, salts or glucose, then send the data to a phone. The field has moved quickly, but almost every new device is a proof of concept. Very few studies test whether the device still gives correct readings after months on a moving, sweating body.

The article proposes a straightforward recipe borrowed from electronics reliability. Pick the numbers that define whether the device works: detection accuracy, electrical continuity, wireless signal strength and so on. Measure them before, during and after an accelerated test, such as a temperature-humidity-bias test or thousands of bending cycles. Call the device failed when one of them drops below a set threshold. Then find the root cause and redesign: a stronger adhesive if layers peel apart, water-repellent outer materials if moisture is the culprit.

2. Printed nanotube humidity sensors that keep working

The printed carbon-nanotube / graphene-oxide humidity sensors described on the Additively Manufactured Electronics page were put through two long-term tests.

  • Sustained high humidity. Twenty printed sensors, eight flat and twelve on curved surfaces, were held at 90% relative humidity for over 400 hours at 40 °C, over 200 hours at 50 °C and over 150 hours at 30 °C. Their readings stayed steady throughout. When the humidity-sensing test was repeated afterwards, they gave the same response as when new: about a 30% change in resistance between 30% and 90% humidity. In repeated wet–dry cycles, each sensor's resistance followed the humidity closely and returned to its starting value every time.
  • Temperature cycling. Six printed traces were cycled between −40 °C and 125 °C (about one hour per cycle, following the JEDEC JESD22-A104 standard) for four months. A trace counted as failed if its resistance at the hot or cold extreme drifted by more than 20%. All six survived the first three months, roughly 2,200 cycles, without significant change. Two began showing open circuits or large resistance jumps in the fourth month.

3. Where printed silver circuits crack

Printed hybrid electronics combine printed features with conventional ones: printed silver lines connecting to copper pads on an ordinary circuit board, for example. This study, presented at EuroSimE 2025, asked how such lines survive repeated heating and cooling, and where they fail.

Aerosol jet printing. The ink is turned into a fine mist, and a stream of gas focuses the mist into a narrow jet that writes lines a few tens of micrometres wide. Because the jet travels a short distance through air, it can print onto steps, slopes and curved parts, not only flat sheets.

The test samples

Each test board carried two copper pads joined by printed features. First a 20 µm layer of a UV-curable insulating polymer (Norland NEA121) was printed, overlapping the copper pads. Then silver-nanoparticle lines were printed on top of it: a narrow line (100 µm wide) and a wide one (500 µm). The boards were baked at 150 °C to fuse (sinter) the silver. The same design was printed on three board materials, chosen because they expand by different amounts when heated: glass-FR4 (the common green circuit board), Kevlar-polyimide and ceramic.

The test

The boards were cycled following the IPC-9701 standard: the Kevlar and ceramic boards between −40 °C and +125 °C, and the FR4 boards on a milder −50 °C to +100 °C profile, because of chamber availability. Each line's resistance was logged every 30 seconds with a four-wire measurement. As cracks start and grow, resistance creeps up; a line was counted as failed once it read more than 1.2 times its starting resistance five times.

What happened

  • Kevlar-polyimide failed first: all 11 lines failed within 1,500 cycles.
  • Ceramic lasted several times longer: 15 of 17 lines failed in about 5,300 cycles.
  • Glass-FR4, on its milder profile, had 21 of 31 lines failed after about 7,000 cycles.
Weibull characteristic life. Failure times in a batch of samples are always spread out. Engineers fit them with a Weibull distribution, a standard statistical model for wear-out. Its "characteristic life" is the number of cycles by which about 63% of the samples are expected to have failed, a single number that makes different designs easy to compare.

Under the microscope, the story was the same on all three boards, for narrow and wide lines alike: almost every failure was a crack in the transition region, where the printed silver, the printed insulator and the edge of the copper pad meet. Cross-sections and electron microscopy confirmed it.

The simulation

To understand why, the sample was modelled with finite element analysis in ABAQUS. Several material properties were measured specifically for the model: how the sintered silver slowly deforms under load (by nano-indentation), how the printed insulator softens with temperature (by dynamic mechanical analysis), and how much each material expands when heated (by thermo-mechanical analysis). The model was cycled through the same temperature profiles, and the strain in the silver was read off once the response had settled.

The simulation put the highest strain exactly where the cracks appeared: at the step in the transition region. It did not yet rank the boards correctly. It predicted the largest strain on ceramic, while in the tests Kevlar failed first. The paper attributes this to simplifications in the preliminary model, which treated Kevlar as having the same stiffness in every direction and left out how the insulator's stiffness changes over time. Both are slated for the follow-up model.

The design lesson. The printed silver line is not the weak link on its own. The weak link is the junction where a printed feature climbs onto a conventional one. The shape of that step, and the mismatch in how the materials around it expand, decide how long the circuit lasts.

What this tells us

  • Reliability has to be designed in and tested. Proof-of-concept results say little about how a device behaves after months of heat, moisture and movement.
  • Printed carbon-nanotube sensors can be robust. They kept their humidity response through hundreds of hours at 90% humidity and about 2,200 harsh temperature cycles.
  • In printed hybrid circuits, junctions fail first. Under the same temperature profile, printed silver lines lasted more than four times longer on ceramic than on Kevlar, and cracks consistently formed at the printed-to-copper transition.

Scope of the work

Sample counts were modest (6 nanotube traces; 11 to 31 silver lines per board), and the FR4 boards ran on a different temperature profile from the other two. The EuroSimE model is explicitly preliminary. Future work outlined in the papers includes acceleration factors between board materials, a fatigue curve for the printed silver, and a fuller test matrix for the nanotube sensors.
