Magnetic Nanoparticle Aggregation and Complete De-encapsulation of Such Aggregates from a Liquid Drop Interior
  • Category: Research
  • Affiliation: University of Maryland, College Park
  • Sponsor: SMIEL Lab
  • PI: Dr. Siddhartha Das
  • Collab: Ghansham Chandel, Vishal Sivasankar, Sai Achyut Madhira
  • Project date: May 2022 - present

Using a magnet to pull a clump of nanoparticles cleanly out of a water drop, with the drop staying round and intact afterwards. One drop can drop off its cargo at several chosen spots in a row, like the nozzle of a tiny printer. Follow-up work shows the released clump can be caught by a second drop.



Put a few tiny particles inside a drop of water and they are trapped. The drop's surface acts like a stretched skin, and for something nanometres across, that skin is an almost unbreakable wall. This project found a way through it. With nothing more than a magnet, the particles inside a drop gather into a clump, push through the surface, and leave the drop completely. The drop then snaps back to its round shape, unharmed, and can do it again somewhere else.

Why this matters

Iron-oxide (Fe3O4) nanoparticles are widely studied for targeted drug delivery and for placing materials precisely where they are needed. They are safe for the body and respond to magnets, so a magnet outside the body or outside a device can steer them.

Liquid drops are a natural way to carry such particles around. Getting the particles out of the drop at the right place is the hard part. Particles inside a drop tend to collect at its surface and stay there, held by surface tension. Many studies have used that effect to make particles line up or self-assemble at the surface. What had essentially not been shown before was the opposite: pulling the particles all the way through the surface and away from the drop, while leaving the drop itself intact. The paper calls this de-encapsulation.

Surface tension, in one line. The molecules at a liquid's surface cling to each other, so the surface behaves like a thin elastic skin. It is the reason a water strider can stand on a pond. For a large object the skin is weak. For a nanoparticle it is enormous compared with any push you can give the particle, so particles reaching the surface usually stay stuck there.

The experiment

The setup is simple to picture:

  • The drop: 10–20 microlitres of water carrying iron-oxide nanoparticles (coated with a polymer called PVP so they disperse well), at five concentrations from 10 to 20 mg per mL.
  • The surrounding liquid: a bath of partly cured silicone (PDMS), roughly as thick as honey, about 4,000 times more viscous than water. It is slightly denser than the drop, so without a magnet the drop would float upward.
  • The magnet: a stack of neodymium bar magnets under the bath. The drop is released 20 mm above it.
  • The cameras: a high-speed camera and a second camera at right angles. Together they record the drop from two sides, which let me reconstruct the size of each clump in 3D.

Looking through a block of silicone bends light, much like a straw looks bent in a glass of water. To keep measurements honest, I photographed a grid inside the silicone and used the distortion to correct every image before taking measurements.

What happens

Once the drop is released close enough to the magnet, the same sequence plays out every time:

  1. The drop sinks toward the magnet, because the pull on the particles outweighs the drop's tendency to float.
  2. The particles gather at the bottom of the drop, the side nearest the magnet. You can see it on video: the top of the drop turns clear while the bottom darkens.
  3. The drop stretches into a teardrop, and a thin neck forms just above the clump.
  4. The clump breaks free. It passes through the drop's surface, pinches off with a little water attached, and settles at the bottom.
  5. The drop returns to its round shape. The thick silicone around it keeps it from deforming permanently.

Why a clump can escape when a single particle can't

This is the heart of the result, and it comes down to how forces scale with size. Four forces act on the clump at the moment it tries to leave: the magnet and gravity pull it down; buoyancy and surface tension hold it back.

The surface-tension grip grows with the clump's size (its width), while the magnetic pull and weight grow with its volume (width cubed). Double the clump's width and the grip doubles, but the pull goes up eightfold. A single nanoparticle is so small that the grip wins by a huge margin. Once enough particles band together, the pull overtakes the grip and the clump punches through. That is why gathering inside the drop is the necessary first step.

What controls it

For each run I measured two things: how far from the magnet the clump broke free, and how big the escaping clump was. Both grew with more particles in the drop and with a bigger drop:

  • The break-free distance rose from roughly 5 mm to about 7.5 mm across the tested range.
  • The escaping clump grew from roughly 0.07 to 0.2 cubic millimetres.

The logic: a drop holding more particles starts forming a clump earlier, further from the magnet. That clump grows big quickly, so it can escape even where the magnetic field is weaker. A bigger clump also faces a bigger surface-tension grip, so the magnetic pull needed to free it rises with it.

One combination did not work: the smallest drop (10 µL) at the lowest concentration (10 mg/mL). It took over 80 seconds to sink and reached the bottom of the container before the clump could pinch off. That points to a minimum amount of magnetic material needed for the effect.

A theoretical model balancing the four forces in Figure 2 was developed with my co-authors. Once two experimental points were used to calibrate two properties that could not be measured directly, the model reproduced the trend of break-free distance across all drop sizes and concentrations.

One drop, three deliveries

To show what this could be used for, I built a container with three magnets on different sides that could be switched on one at a time. A single drop (20 µL, 20 mg/mL) was released and steered from magnet to magnet:

Each time a magnet was switched on, the drop travelled toward it and released a clump, then stayed intact for the next leg. Every release left the drop with fewer particles, so it moved more slowly each time and had to get closer to the magnet before the next clump could escape: 11.75 mm, then 5.33 mm, then 2.71 mm. In effect, the drop behaved like the nozzle of a printer that deposits particles on command.

Part 2: Handing the cargo to another drop

The natural next question: if a drop can release its particles on command, can a second drop catch them? In follow-up work, now posted as a preprint, we showed that it can. The clump that leaves one drop, the source drop, can be steered straight into a second drop, the receiver drop, and both drops come out of it intact.

How it works

The receiver drop holds almost no particles (less than 0.01 mg per mL) and rests at the bottom of the silicone bath, just above the magnet. The particle-rich source drop is released 15–18.5 mm higher, directly above it, and the whole bath sits on a stack of neodymium magnets with a peak field of about 220 mT. As in Part 1, the source drop sinks toward the magnet, stretches, and lets go of a clump. Because the two drops are lined up, the falling clump lands on top of the receiver drop, pushes its surface inward and breaks through. The receiver drop flattens for a moment, then relaxes back to a near-spherical shape, a little larger than before, with no noticeable loss of particles into the silicone.

Camera tracking of the source drop tells the same story from the outside. Its centre speeds up as it approaches the magnet, then slows sharply the moment the clump leaves, because it has just lost most of the magnetic material that was pulling it down.

Why the drops have to be placed just right. To get into the receiver drop, the clump has to push through the same kind of surface-tension "skin" it escaped from. Two things help it. First, the receiver drop should sit as close to the magnet as possible, where the magnetic pull is strongest. Second, the clump should be released far enough above the receiver that it has time to pick up speed on the way down. If the magnetic pull alone can't beat the surface, the clump's momentum can still carry it through. A simple force balance in the preprint captures both conditions.

A drop that is different at the top and the bottom

Once inside the receiver drop, the clump is suddenly in thin water instead of thick silicone, and it reorganises. The particles form upright chains at the bottom of the drop, which we call magnetic skyscrapers. Switch the magnet off and the skyscrapers dissolve into a dense magnetic liquid layer sitting at the bottom of the drop, with nearly clear water above it. The receiver drop has become heterogeneous: its contents are no longer uniform.

  • On and off. Switching the field on rebuilds the skyscrapers; switching it off melts them back into the liquid layer. The same behaviour repeated over multiple on–off cycles.
  • Changing direction. When the field was tilted to one side or the other, the skyscrapers always lined up with it.
  • A moving field. When the field was swept back and forth, the magnetic liquid flowed along the inside wall of the drop and back into the middle. The drop's surface acts as a soft, flexible container wall.

The receiver drop also comes away with the source drop's "magnetic activity": once it holds enough particles, it can be steered by a magnet itself, which it could not before. And because neither drop is permanently damaged, one source drop can in principle supply several receivers and then be reloaded by becoming a receiver itself.

What this tells us

  • The surface barrier can be beaten with size, not force. Letting the particles clump first makes the magnetic pull grow faster than the surface-tension grip.
  • It is predictable. Particle concentration and drop size set where the release happens and how much material comes out, and a force-balance model captures the trend.
  • It is repeatable from one drop. The drop survives each release, so a single drop can deliver cargo to several locations.
  • The cargo can be handed on. A released clump can be caught by a second drop, carrying its magnetic behaviour with it, and the receiving drop becomes a two-layer system with its own magnetic dynamics.
  • It points to applications such as carrying particle cargo through microchannels, controlled drug release, and on-demand material deposition for printing.

Scope of the study

All experiments used a very viscous silicone bath, which is what keeps the drop from deforming permanently. Showing the same effect in thinner surroundings such as oil or air, which practical printing and delivery would need, is left for future work. The model also relies on two calibrated constants rather than independently measured ones. The drop-to-drop transfer work (Part 2) is described here from its public preprint, which has not yet been peer reviewed; the transfer conditions in it are explained qualitatively.

My role

I designed and ran the experiments: preparing the nanoparticle dispersions and the silicone bath, building the magnet and two-camera imaging setup with its refraction correction, measuring release distances and clump volumes, and setting up the three-magnet delivery demonstration. I also wrote the first draft of the paper. The theoretical model was developed by my co-authors Ghansham Chandel and Vishal Sivasankar, under the supervision of Prof. Siddhartha Das, who conceived the problem. I presented this work at the Burgers Symposium at George Washington University in May 2025.

For the drop-to-drop transfer study (Part 2), I am the first author: I designed the experiments and the setup, analysed the data and wrote most of the paper, working with Sai Achyut Madhira under the supervision of Prof. Siddhartha Das.
