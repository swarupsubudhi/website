Cost Optimisation: Value Engineering (VAVE) Case Studies
  • Category: Professional
  • Affiliation: Tata Motors Ltd., Jamshedpur (Transmission factory)
  • Mentor: Mr. Sabas Fernando, Team Lead, NPI Transmissions (2017)
  • Team: Sabas Fernando, Ravi Pathak (2017); Roop Sengupta, Launch, and Shashank Singh, PPC (2019)
  • Project date: May - Nov 2017; May - Nov 2019

Two value engineering studies on truck transmissions, each presented at the INVEST national value engineering conference: re-sourcing a tipper truck's hydraulic pump, and changing the friction material in a gearbox's synchronisers.



Value engineering asks a simple question of every part in a product: what does it actually do, and is it worth what we pay for it? At Tata Motors' transmission factory I worked on two such studies. The first found that a pump bought as part of a big supplier kit was quietly costing far more than it should, and that the way it was fitted wasted gearbox oil on every truck. The second looked for a better friction material inside the gearbox's synchronisers.

Value analysis / value engineering (VAVE). Every part has a job, described as an action and an object: a cylinder lifts the load body, a hose carries oil. Value engineering lists those jobs, works out what each one costs, and looks for jobs that cost more than they are worth. Ideas are then generated freely, scored on feasibility and benefit, developed, approved, implemented and finally audited to confirm the savings are real.

Case study 1: The hydraulic tipping kit (2017)

The problem

Tipper trucks carry rock, ore and construction material, and tip their load by raising the body with a hydraulic cylinder. The LPK 2518 tipper's tipping kit (the cylinder, oil tank, pump and hoses) was bought from the load-body supplier as a single kit. Because it arrived as one package, nobody had ever value-engineered the parts inside it. Meanwhile, the load-body team was complaining about wasted gearbox oil and scrapped parts at the vehicle assembly line.

How the tipping kit is powered. A power take-off (PTO) unit bolts onto the gearbox and draws power from one of its shafts. It drives a hydraulic pump, which pushes oil into the cylinder to raise the body. Releasing the pressure lets the body come back down.

What was going wrong on the line

The gearbox left the transmission factory with a PTO and a blanking end cover. At the vehicle factory, operators removed and scrapped that end cover, fitted the kit supplier's pump in its place (about 15 minutes), and topped up roughly 2 litres of gearbox oil lost in the process (another 15 minutes). The work was duplicated, the oil was wasted, and the scrapped cover was pure loss.

Finding where the value was hiding

The team broke the kit's cost down part by part and calculated a value index for each, leaving out parts that couldn't be changed because of fitment or supplier constraints, such as the load body and the cylinder. Two parts stood out: the gear pump and the delivery hoses.

The key insight came from comparing the same pump in two contexts. Inside the big tipping kit, it was a small slice of the total. Inside a gearbox assembly, the same pump was nearly three times as large a share of the cost. In a large kit, the supplier naturally focuses value engineering on the big items (the body and cylinder), and smaller but expensive parts slip through unexamined.

Ideas and scoring

Each kit part's function was written as a verb and a noun (the cylinder lifts the load body, the PTO and pump provide power, the hoses facilitate oil movement), and mapped in a FAST diagram. Brainstorming produced six ideas, which were scored out of 50 on technology readiness, chance of implementation, development cost, time to implement and cost benefit:

The top three went to a weighted evaluation. A paired comparison set the weights: cost reduction potential and operator safety at 31% each, ease of assembly at 23% and low wastage at 15%. The winner, with a weighted score of 346 against 284 and 200 for the other two, was to develop an alternate supplier for a combined PTO and pump, fitted directly at the transmission factory.

Making it happen

A tender was floated, and the company already supplying the PTO offered to supply the PTO and pump together. Buying pumps for several tipper models from one source, instead of inside each kit, also brought volume pricing. After management approval, the change went through design analysis and drawing release, prototype trials and quality sign-off, field trials with feedback from the load-body team, fitment trials on every sub-model, supplier ramp-up, and a planned run-out of the old kit stock. The new pump met its target of no more than 5% performance loss in continuous running at 180 bar, on the test bench and in vehicle trials.

Results

  • Cost: an audited ₹4.73 crore per year in savings on the LPK 2518, from lower part cost plus the eliminated process steps. Rolled out across the tipper family (LPK 2518, 2518 HD and 2523 in production, with LPK 3118 in progress), the projected total was ₹13.51 crore per year.
  • Quality and safety: no more opening the gearbox and refilling oil at the vehicle line, and an easier, more ergonomic operation.
  • Delivery and environment: shorter cycle time, and less gearbox oil wasted and disposed of.

The hardest parts were not technical: developing the new supplier, getting every department to sign off, and ramping up volumes at the supplier.

Case study 2: G1150 gearbox synchronisers (2019)

What a synchroniser does. In a manual gearbox, the gear you are about to select is spinning at a different speed from the shaft it must lock onto. A synchroniser presses a cone-shaped ring against the gear before engagement, and friction between the two matches their speeds so the gears mesh without grinding. The friction surface on that cone decides how smooth the shift is and how long the synchroniser lasts.

The G1150 is a nine-speed truck gearbox family rated for 1,150 Nm of input torque (1,300 Nm in its overdrive version), used in heavy vehicles with roughly 180 to 230 hp engines. This study examined its synchronising cones and rings.

Function and cost

The cones and rings were broken down by machining operation, and the function of each feature was written as a verb and a noun. A FAST diagram mapped the critical path of functions, and a cost-worth analysis compared what each function cost against what it was worth. Features made in a single machining operation were grouped, since their cost can't be separated.

Ideas and selection

Brainstorming with design, manufacturing and quality produced five ideas, scored out of 50 on the same five feasibility criteria:

  • Woven carbon lining instead of molybdenum-coated synchro cones: 36
  • Embedded carbon lining instead of molybdenum-coated cones: 35
  • Eliminating the moon-milling operation on the synchro ring: 32
  • Reducing cone lug width, which reduces machining on the engaging gear: 30
  • Outsourcing the hobbing and V-chamfering of teeth: 30

The top three went to a weighted evaluation on ease of assembly, cost reduction potential, synchroniser life and low wastage. Woven carbon lining came out on top. Because the overall design barely changed and the manufacturing process stayed similar, the new part was quick and inexpensive to develop.

Validation and rollout

  • Stress analysis (CAE): within specification.
  • Prototype trials: fitment on the synchro ring and main-shaft sub-assembly OK, cone gap OK, compatibility with the 80W90 gearbox oil OK, gearbox noise and load tests within specification.
  • Field trials: zero complaints from 25 gearboxes.
  • Assembly trials: sub-assembly fitment, flushing and load testing OK.

After a successful launch, the change was considered for the G600, G750 and G950 gearbox families as well.

What this tells us

  • Kits hide cost. A part that is minor inside a large purchased kit can be a major cost on its own, and nobody owns its value.
  • Process waste counts. Some of the tipping-kit savings came from removing steps and scrap, not just a cheaper part.
  • Structured scoring makes decisions defensible. Weighted matrices let a cross-functional team agree on one proposal and take it to management.
